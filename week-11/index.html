An environmental economics proposal by Michael Scholz

No green claim without the receipts.

Companies can call a product green because most customers cannot check the mine, factory, power grid, shipping route, and landfill themselves. My idea is simple: if a company wants to profit from an environmental claim, it has to show the full calculation.

RECEIPTS ATTACHED
The one-look version

Create a Federal Office of Environmental Claims.

The office would not decide which products people are allowed to buy. It would regulate voluntary claims such as green, sustainable, carbon neutral, zero emission, and climate friendly. No claim would be allowed without a public environmental receipt.

01

Make the boundary visible

A company must show whether it counted raw materials, production, shipping, product use, and disposal.

02

Compare against something real

“Better” only means something if the company names the alternative, time period, location, and assumptions.

03

Put the risk on the claimant

The company pays for verification. False claims bring refunds, penalties, and a temporary loss of claim privileges.

Try the rule

What would a company actually have to prove?

Different claims need different evidence. The goal is not to force every product into one score. It is to stop a company from using a narrow fact to imply a much broader environmental benefit.

This is a demonstration of the proposed rule, not a legal determination about a particular company.

Receipts required

    Why economics matters

    The product is not the only thing being sold. So is the story.

    Environmental quality is often a credence attribute. Even after buying a product, the customer may never know whether the environmental story was true. That information gap creates a market where a vague claim can be cheaper than making a real improvement.

    Information asymmetry

    The seller knows more

    A customer cannot cheaply trace every supplier. A firm can therefore benefit from information that the buyer cannot verify.

    Bad incentive

    Marketing can beat improvement

    If a green-looking claim raises demand, a firm may spend on wording and packaging instead of reducing its actual impact.

    Proposed correction

    Make proof part of the price

    Verification raises the cost of making a claim, but it also lets honest firms separate themselves from firms that only imitate them.

    The changed margin: A firm would compare the profit from making a green claim with the cost of measuring, verifying, and defending it. Claims that cannot survive that test should disappear. Real improvements become more valuable because competitors cannot copy the language for almost nothing.

    52%

    Why boundaries matter

    A 2024 Department of Energy life-cycle estimate found a small electric SUV produced 52 percent fewer greenhouse-gas emissions than a comparable gasoline SUV. That is a meaningful advantage, but it is not the same thing as zero impact.

    What exists now

    This idea builds on a fence that already has a purpose.

    The FTC already says environmental marketing claims need reliable scientific support, but its Green Guides are guidance rather than a single mandatory receipt system. USDA protects the meaning of “organic.” EPA, DOE, and other agencies maintain specialized methods and labels. Those separate programs exist because food, cars, appliances, and chemicals require different expertise.

    A new office should not erase those systems. It should create a common front door and public format, then rely on the existing technical agencies for the actual standards. That is my Chesterton's Fence concern: centralizing the claim might simplify information, but centralizing every scientific method could destroy useful specialization.

    What changes

    Voluntary claim, mandatory proof.

    A company could still sell a product without a green receipt. It simply could not make covered environmental claims. That keeps the rule focused on speech used for profit instead of turning the agency into a general product regulator.

    The office would start with high-risk, broad claims and large companies. Narrow factual claims such as “30 percent recycled plastic” would use a shorter verification path. Broad claims such as “sustainable” would face the strongest burden because they can imply benefits across many environmental categories.

    Strongest arguments

    The idea can help consumers and still create new problems.

    The serious question is not whether greenwashing is annoying. It is whether a federal verification system improves decisions enough to justify its cost, delay, and political risk.

    The strongest case for it

    • Consumers get comparable information they could not efficiently produce alone.
    • Honest companies are less likely to lose business to firms making cheaper, vague claims.
    • Companies must consider impacts hidden upstream or shifted to another community.
    • A public method makes assumptions visible and easier for researchers to challenge.
    • Fees place more of the cost on firms choosing to profit from the claim.

    The strongest case against it

    • Life-cycle calculations can produce false precision because suppliers and future product use are uncertain.
    • Compliance costs may protect large firms and discourage small firms from discussing real improvements.
    • Political pressure may determine which assumptions count as official science.
    • Approval delays could slow products that actually reduce environmental harm.
    • A government receipt may create too much trust in a number that is conditional and incomplete.
    Guardrails

    How I would keep the cure from becoming worse than the problem.

    Show a range

    Publish uncertainty and sensitivity tests instead of pretending every impact is known exactly.

    Use tiers

    Simple factual claims receive a cheap path. Broad superiority and net-zero claims receive a full audit.

    Protect small firms

    Offer fee waivers, open calculators, and shared product-category data so compliance does not become a barrier to entry.

    Audit the auditor

    Randomly recheck approved claims, rotate verifiers, publish conflicts, and penalize both the firm and verifier for knowing misstatements.

    Do not confuse categories

    Organic is a production standard. It should not silently become a climate claim unless the climate evidence is separately shown.

    Expire every receipt

    A claim must be updated when suppliers, energy sources, product design, or the comparison product materially changes.

    Environmental justice

    The receipt should show where the cost lands.

    A national average can hide a mine, factory, waste site, or polluted electricity source affecting one community. The receipt should therefore include a location map for major impacts and report which stage creates the largest local exposure.

    This would not automatically fix unequal exposure. It would at least make it harder for a company to advertise the clean consumer experience while hiding costs imposed on workers or communities somewhere else in the supply chain.

    Unknowns

    What I still would not know.

    Would shoppers use the receipt, or would it become another label people ignore? Would firms improve products or simply stop making claims? Would shared databases reduce costs, or allow one bad assumption to spread everywhere? Would foreign suppliers disclose enough information? The pilot should measure changes in claims, prices, market shares, emissions, approval time, and small-business participation before the system expands.
