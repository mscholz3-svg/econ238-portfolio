# Building Faster Without Building Blind

America needs new transmission lines, nuclear plants, renewable generation, mines, and other infrastructure to build a cleaner economy. The problem is that the rules meant to prevent careless construction can also make responsible projects slow, expensive, and unpredictable. Our conversation asks whether the United States can build faster without losing environmental review, public participation, and legal accountability.

## Written Synthesis

Potter shows how fear of lawsuits pushes agencies to write extremely long NEPA documents. Howard argues that detailed rules have replaced judgment. Wang connects both problems to a lawyerly American culture that protects procedure but struggles to build. Doomberg argues that litigation has become an industry. Together, they suggest that changing one environmental rule will help, but will not fix the larger problem. [1, 8, 9, 10]

I do not think the answer is removing environmental review or simply having fewer lawyers. I would give one lead agency responsibility for each major project and set a firm deadline for federal approvals. Officials could no longer delay a difficult decision by passing responsibility to another agency. People could still raise environmental harms and challenge decisions outside the agency's authority, but they would have fewer chances to stop a project over minor procedural disputes.

The immediate effect should be a faster, more predictable review. Lower delay risk would also reduce financing costs and make more transmission lines, nuclear plants, renewable projects, and mines worth building. The same reform, however, could speed up fossil fuel projects or projects that harm nearby communities. People living next to a project may lose influence, while the benefits are spread across a larger region. That makes public trust part of the economic tradeoff.

One point my partner made that changed how I thought about the issue was that the government could offer financial assistance to communities willing to host nuclear power plants. Communities that strongly oppose a plant could say no, while places that value the assistance may be more willing to accept the local costs. I agree with the idea, but the additional assistance would make nuclear projects even more expensive and could put the United States further behind countries that can build them more quickly and cheaply.

Even with faster review, the lead agency should publish its evidence, explain the tradeoffs, respond to major local concerns, and measure the results after construction. That gives the public a way to judge the decision instead of having to trust the agency without evidence. Social media can expose mistakes, but it can also reward the most alarming version of a story before the facts are clear.

I think America needs to become somewhat more engineering oriented without copying China's authoritarian system. NEPA reform removes one bottleneck, but permitting, litigation, grid connections, agency staffing, and local opposition work like an O-ring system: one failure can stop the project. I am still unsure whether limiting procedural challenges can reduce delay without allowing real environmental harm to be ignored. Evidence comparing approval times, environmental results, and public participation under the new agency rules would change how confident I am in this reform.
