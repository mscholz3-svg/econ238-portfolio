Econ 238 · Extreme weather risk

More people. More exposure. Fewer storm deaths per person.

U.S. fatalities from tornadoes, floods and hurricanes have fallen sharply relative to population since the 1940s—even as migration has added people to exposed coastal counties. That is evidence of adaptation, not immunity.

The central finding

A larger country became safer per person

The annual average across the full 1940–2025 period was 240 combined deaths from the three NWS categories. Raw totals remain volatile because one disaster can dominate a decade. Population-adjusted risk shows the longer movement more clearly.

What the charts establish: recorded deaths fell relative to population. They do not establish that storms weakened, that climate change has no effect, or that adaptation caused the entire decline.

Economics of risk
Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability

Policy can reduce the hazard, reduce exposure, or make a given exposure less deadly. Forecasts, shelters, stronger buildings, medical care, electricity and communications can lower vulnerability. Land-use rules and relocation can lower exposure. The efficient mix depends on the marginal lives saved per dollar and on who bears the costs.

Why people still move toward risk

Risk is only one part of the choice

Households also value jobs, housing, taxes, amenities, family and warm weather. Moving to an exposed place can therefore be rational even when the hazard is real. But subsidized insurance or disaster aid can shift part of the expected cost to others, weakening the incentive to avoid risk.

Migration and hurricane exposure

Some domestic migration has moved toward the South Atlantic coast

Census county estimates show that several coastal counties repeatedly ranked among the strongest domestic-migration destinations. This raises exposure, but the evidence is descriptive: a coastal address is not a precise measure of hurricane risk.

A measurement trap

“Heat deaths” can mean two very different things

≈120,000Burke et al. estimate U.S. deaths statistically attributable each year to temperatures above or below the mortality-minimizing range.

The Burke estimate is not a count of death certificates labeled “heat” or “cold.” It uses changes in all-cause mortality across temperatures and includes deaths that appear clinically as cardiovascular disease, accidents and other causes. The paper estimates roughly 5% of U.S. deaths are attributable to nonoptimal temperature and finds cold causes more deaths than heat in every setting studied. Both measures can be useful, but combining them would be misleading.

Economic implication: adaptation should target actual exposure and vulnerability, not only the deaths that receive a weather label. Burke et al. find that many popular interventions lack strong causal evidence. Weatherization has better evidence for reducing cold-related illness than many awareness programs, but even there the cost, targeting and measured health benefit matter.

The broader evidence

Exposure drives losses

The IPCC SREX report concludes with high confidence that increasing exposure of people and economic assets has been the major cause of long-run increases in weather- and climate-disaster losses. Dollar losses are not fatalities, but the mechanism is the same: where and how we build changes the damage created by a hazard.

But wait

Fewer deaths are not the whole welfare calculation

Mortality can fall while property losses, displacement or unequal burdens rise. Reporting standards changed, extreme years remain important, and the NWS hurricane column counts wind fatalities rather than every death associated with a tropical cyclone. The 2020s are also incomplete.

The question economics adds

The useful question is not whether danger exists. It is which combination of warnings, building standards, infrastructure, insurance pricing and land-use choices reduces risk at the lowest social cost without hiding who pays and who remains exposed.
