Beer, wine, and spirits

The Climate Cost of a Drink

A liter of wine creates more greenhouse gas emissions than a liter of beer, but people do not drink them in the same amount. I wanted to see what happens when the comparison is one equal U.S. standard drink.

3.5×

In this comparison, a standard glass of wine has about three and a half times the emissions of a standard pour of spirits.

The question

If beer, wine, and spirits contain the same amount of alcohol, do they have the same climate cost?

1 · Make the comparison fair

One drink means three different volumes.

The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism defines each of these as about 14 grams of pure alcohol. That gives the comparison one common unit.

Beer

12 fl oz at 5%

Wine

5 fl oz at 12%

Spirits

1.5 fl oz at 40%

Same alcohol: about 14 grams of pure ethanol in each serving.

2 · Read the result carefully

Per liter and per drink answer different questions.

Spirits are emissions intensive per liter because distilling takes energy. A standard pour is only 1.5 ounces, though, so the serving size changes the comparison.

This is not a recommendation to switch drinks.

The chart only compares estimated climate emissions for the same amount of alcohol. It says nothing about health, price, taste, or how much someone may actually pour.

A cocktail can also add mixers, ice, refrigeration, and more packaging that are not represented by a plain 1.5 ounce pour.

52 kg

per person per year

This was the average alcohol related footprint in the Swedish study population. It was about 3% of their diet related greenhouse gas emissions. It is context for that group, not a global average. [1]

3 · The container can change the answer

The same beer did not have one fixed footprint.

A separate Italian life cycle study compared one pale lager in different packages. I scaled each result to a 12 ounce serving so the packaging options use the same amount of beer.

4 · A little context

How does alcohol compare with everyday drinks?

I added a few familiar drinks because 290 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent is hard to picture on its own. Each number below represents one common serving.

What I learned

Before doing this I thought distilling would automatically make liquor the worst option. Per liter it is much more emissions intensive than beer, but a standard serving is also much smaller. Once I compared equal amounts of alcohol, wine and beer came out much higher per drink. I also did not expect the container to matter enough to change the beer result by almost three times in one study. The main thing I learned is that the answer depends a lot on what unit is being compared.

How I calculated it

grams per drink = kg CO₂e per liter × serving size in liters × 1,000

Example for wine: 2.16 kg CO₂e per liter × 0.14787 liters × 1,000 = 319 grams CO₂e, rounded to 320 grams.

Important limits

  • The main life cycle values represent beverages consumed in Sweden, while the serving sizes are U.S. standard drinks.
  • Production methods, packaging, imports, and electricity sources vary. These are estimates, not labels for every brand.
  • The beer point uses 0.81 kg CO₂e per liter, the upper end of the source's 0.73 to 0.81 beer range.
  • Actual pours can contain more than one standard drink.
  • The result does not include a person's travel to a store, restaurant, or bar.
